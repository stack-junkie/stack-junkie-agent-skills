# Let the reader's task decide the article's length

An article should be long enough to do its job and short enough to avoid doing a different one. That is the editorial position argued here. A word target can help commission a piece, but it should not decide which explanation survives the final edit.

Google says it has no preferred word count. That removes one common search rationale for padding, but it does not settle the craft question of how much explanation a particular reader needs. [Google's people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

## The missing step matters more than the count

Imagine a guide that tells a reader to paste a command, then announces success without explaining the result. Adding an expected output and a recovery branch would make it longer. Those additions would also be the first things this editorial standard asks for.

Now imagine the same guide opening with several paragraphs about the history of computing. If the task is a small configuration repair, that introduction has to justify the delay. The question is what it contributes to this reader at this point in the work.

These are constructed cases, not reports of reader testing. They show the principle: length is a consequence of the explanation, and the explanation needs a purpose.

## Why editors still need a planning range

Editors need constraints. A commission with no size limit can become difficult to budget, review or place. A writer can also defend every detour as essential context.

Keep a planning range when it helps coordinate the work. Pair it with a clear account of the reader, task and expected depth. If the draft exceeds the range, ask which section changed the scope and whether that section belongs here. The answer may be a shorter article, a focused companion piece or a justified longer commission.

## A better finishing question

Before delivery, ask what the reader can now understand or accomplish. Then check what remains unexplained, repeated or irrelevant. That review can support a specific revision: add the missing condition, remove the repeated conclusion, or give an example enough space to make sense.

A draft that reaches its target and leaves the reader guessing is unfinished. A draft that meets the reader's need should not acquire another section merely to reach a round number. End when the argument or task is complete.
